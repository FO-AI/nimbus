## Where this touches F&O

Anything your unit publishes: an intranet page, a policy PDF, a slide deck
sent to the whole division, a form, a newsletter. AI can speed all of it up
and can also quietly make it unusable for someone with a screen reader.

## What AI is genuinely useful for

- A **first pass** at alt text for a photo or a simple chart
- **Draft captions** on a recorded session, as a base for correction
- **Plain-language rewrites** of dense policy text — often a real accessibility
  gain, since complexity is a barrier too
- Finding structural problems: missing headings, tables used for layout

## Where it fails

**Alt text describes pixels, not purpose.** A generated description of a
budget chart says "a bar chart with blue bars." What a reader needs is the
finding: "Travel and Supplies account for 80% of the FY26 overspend." Only you
know why the image is in the document.

**Captions get proper nouns wrong.** ConnectCarolina, InfoPorte, Onyen,
people's names, fund numbers — the terms that carry the meaning are the ones
auto-captioning misses. Uncorrected captions are the most common accessibility
complaint about recorded meetings.

**Fluent output hides missing structure.** AI-drafted text reads well and
routinely arrives with no heading levels, link text that says "click here,"
and colour used as the only way to tell a status apart.

## The rule

A human reviews AI-generated accessibility content before it is published.
Alt text, captions, and summaries are drafts until someone who knows the
subject has checked them.

## Before you publish

- Does the alt text say what the image is **for**?
- Have captions been corrected for names and University system names?
- Do headings nest properly, and does link text describe its destination?
- Does anything rely on colour alone?

The [Digital Accessibility Office](https://digitalaccessibility.unc.edu/)
reviews University material and will look at a document if you are unsure.
